Getting ever better in communicating with agents is **the** skill. Tools are
changing even as we gain work experience wiht agents. Intentionally asking
your agents for ways to improve is one angle and that is a great way to learn
that I have posted about in the past.

Since we challenge **everything** in this new era, I'm also challenging the
premise that I need to work on the things where someone else says I need to
improve. WHile that's still important, it can take me of course from the skills
that I have developed through my life. Meaning, don't throw away the good things
that I have done in the past and learn a totally new system. Build on what I've done.

## Set it up with a prompt

I prompted GitHub Copilot which is the agent workspace I use thorugh
GitHub Copiot CLI or Microsoft Scout to do many tasks in my day job. It is
similar in ways that I have prompted in the past asking for opportunities to
learn, but you'll see the different tack.

> Identify the attributes of human-agent communication. in light of our
> communication, analyze all my sessions with you in github copilot and scout
> and score me on those attributes. give me a table with scores, sorted with
> best at top. and below include a narrative about that output. I am doing this
> to encourage the continued use of skilsl I have developed that are effective

## Spend time reviewing the output

It'll use tokens, so make sure you're not doing this to check a box. Spend time
with the outcome. Because of the nature of it, if you have had any success
whatsoever in getting agents to do work for you, it should make you feel good
about the skills you've developed.

Give it a try and let me know how it goes by messaging me on LinkedIn. Here's mine.

## Hoopdad: Human-Agent Communication Assessment

Date: October 17, 2026

**Your strongest human-agent communication skill is turning one successful interaction into a reusable way of working.** You do not merely ask for an answer: you often improve the skill, instructions, test process, or handoff so future work benefits. That is the habit most worth deliberately continuing.

**Coverage and limits:** I inventoried **3,572 local Copilot and Scout session records containing 25,198 stored turns**, spanning late April-October 17, 2026, and checked hosted history reaching April 15. I scanned the stored user-message fields for filtering, then closely reviewed a **79-turn core sample across 60 sessions**, supplemented by targeted follow-ups and hosted examples. Automated prompts and material with unclear authorship were not credited as your personal writing. **This is a cross-session assessment, not a line-by-line audit of every transcript.**

Scores are judgment-based, not a standardized test: **9 = repeatedly demonstrated strength; 8 = strong but variable; 7 = effective, with recurring opportunities for greater precision.** I assessed meaning and usefulness, not spelling or typing polish.

### What the scores mean

**You communicate as someone designing a working relationship with an agent, not just submitting isolated prompts.** The strongest recurring pattern is:

**Describe the intended result -> inspect what happened -> challenge a mismatch -> preserve the lesson.**

The last step is especially valuable. In Scout on August 7, you asked that challenges encountered during a presentation-building run become improvements to the underlying skill. In Copilot on September 14, you asked for a compact repository-navigation skill and an instruction that would carry across sessions. Those are concrete examples of making today's effort useful tomorrow. **Keep asking, "What should we change in the skill so the next run works better?"**

Your outcome framing is another strength to retain. The deployment example works because it makes success recognizable from the user's perspective. Similarly, "never output text as a graphic" explains a practical requirement, editability, that an agent might otherwise sacrifice for appearance. **You are particularly effective when you state both the desired behavior and why it matters.**

Your corrective questions also deserve reinforcement. Asking whether a blockage is **permission or feasibility** forces a useful distinction. Asking why an agent used the wrong source exposes a process defect. These are better interventions than simply asking it to "try harder." Importantly, **many of these corrections were responses to agent failures; their necessity is not evidence that you communicated poorly.**

The lower scores describe the cost of your speed, not a need to become more formal. You often communicate in compact, high-context messages while several workstreams are active. That is efficient when context is shared. It becomes less reliable when a new session must reconstruct ownership, priorities, or what "done" means.

The smallest useful adjustment is to add a short closing instruction to substantial requests:

> **First achieve X. Preserve Y. Stop when Z is verified. Ask me only if decision A is required.**

That would strengthen prioritization and stopping conditions without sacrificing your natural style.

**The behavior to preserve is your combination of practical outcomes, evidence-based challenge, and reusable learning.** Simplified Technical English could make the action boundaries clearer, but it should support those existing strengths, not replace your curiosity, judgment, or conversational speed.
